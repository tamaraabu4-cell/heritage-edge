The war between the US, Israel and Iran began on 28 February. Nine months later, the Strait of Hormuz is still largely closed, talks keep starting and stalling, and the market effects have spread far beyond oil.

This post isn't about the politics. It's about what the war did to the things I follow most closely: oil, gold, currencies, other commodities, and Nigeria. Figures are as of 11 November unless I say otherwise.

## Why one strait matters so much

Imagine a town where one road carries a fifth of all the food. Close it, and prices don't just rise for the people on that road. They rise everywhere, because everyone is now competing for what's left.

The Strait of Hormuz is that road. It's the narrow channel out of the Gulf, and about a fifth of the world's oil normally passes through it, along with a large share of its liquefied natural gas (LNG), mostly from Qatar. Within hours of the first strikes, Iran warned ships not to pass. Traffic fell by about 70%, insurers pulled cover, and by April around 2,000 ships were stranded inside the Gulf.

## Oil: a rollercoaster driven by headlines

Brent, the main global oil price, started the war at around $70 a barrel. Here's the ride since:

- **8 March:** above $100 for the first time in four years.
- **11 March:** the 32 members of the International Energy Agency agree to release 400 million barrels from emergency stocks, about four days of global use. It barely dented the price.
- **8 April:** a temporary ceasefire knocks Brent down more than 13% in a day. It isn't put into practice.
- **30 April:** $126 during the day, the high for the year so far.
- **1 July:** down to about $72 after a peace memorandum is signed, back where it started.
- **July:** the truce collapses, the strait closes again, and Brent goes back towards $100.
- **30 October:** $118, after the first known limpet-mine attack on a tanker in the strait and Houthi attacks on Saudi oil sites.
- **4 November:** down almost 9% to $94.62 in a day, as traders bet on peace after the midterms, as I wrote in my [Bank of England post](/blog/bank-of-england-raises-rates.html).
- **11 November:** about $94, after Iran denied any talks were happening. Still around 50% higher than a year ago.

My take: the price was never really about how much oil was missing on a given day. It was about how likely traders thought it was that the strait would reopen soon. Every ceasefire headline moved that probability, and the price moved with it. It's the same idea I use in my notes: markets move on surprises, not on news.

## Gold: the war that made gold fall

This was the part that surprised me most. Wars are supposed to be good for gold. It's the classic "safe" asset.

And it did jump at first. Gold had already hit a record of about $5,600 an ounce in January. When the war started, it spiked above $5,300 within hours. But then it fell, and kept falling. By late April it was more than 10% below where the war began. Today it's around $4,000, roughly a quarter lower.

The reason is a chain:

1. The war pushes oil up.
2. Higher oil means higher inflation everywhere.
3. Central banks that were expected to *cut* interest rates now have to think about *raising* them. The Bank of England already has, and traders now expect the Fed to raise rates in December.
4. Gold pays no interest, so when interest rates rise, holding it costs you more in lost interest.
5. Higher US rates also make the dollar stronger, and a stronger dollar makes gold more expensive for everyone else.

Some investors also sold gold simply to raise cash when other things were falling.

These are the same two forces I wrote about in my [midterms post](/blog/what-the-midterms-did-to-the-dollar.html), when gold jumped 3% on a weaker dollar and lower rates. The war ran them in reverse, for months.

I also need to correct myself. In that post, I said gold had "been bouncing around $4,050 for the past year". Compared with a year ago, that's roughly true. But it hides a huge round trip: up to $5,600 in January and back down again. A flat line between two dates can hide a lot.

One thing hasn't changed: central banks are still buying. They bought 61 tonnes in September, their biggest month of the year. Some of the biggest buyers aren't trading the next rate decision. They're thinking in decades.

## Currencies: who buys the oil, and who sells it

In an oil shock, currencies split into two groups.

**Oil importers lost.** Every barrel costs more, so more of their money flows abroad to pay for energy.
- **The euro and the pound** both fell against the dollar when the war began, even though their bond yields rose.
- **The Japanese yen** slid towards 160 per dollar. Japan imports almost all its oil, and it spent over $30 billion trying to support the yen.
- **Asian emerging markets** were hit hardest. By May, the Indonesian rupiah had hit a record low, and the Indian rupee and Philippine peso had hit historic lows.

**The dollar won.** It's the world's safe-haven currency, and the US produces most of its own oil, so it's less exposed to the shock. Today it's at its highest level since April 2025, as traders price in that December rate rise.

**Oil exporters, like Canada and Norway,** were expected to hold up best, because higher oil prices mean more money coming in.

My take: if you want to know how a currency will react to an oil shock, don't start with the war. Start with the country's energy bill. That one question sorts most currencies into the right group.

## Other commodities: it's not just oil

The strait carries much more than crude.

- **Gas:** Qatar, one of the world's biggest LNG exporters, stopped production on 2 March. European gas prices doubled within days, from about €30 to over €60 per megawatt hour, before settling lower.
- **Fertiliser:** about a third of the world's fertiliser shipments pass through Hormuz. Urea, the most widely used nitrogen fertiliser, rose from about $400–490 a tonne before the war to around $700 by April.
- **Jet fuel:** up nearly 84% by May. European airline shares fell about 14% in the first months of the year while the wider market rose.
- **Shipping:** war-risk insurance became four to six times more expensive in the first week alone.

The fertiliser story is the one I'd watch longest. Farmers who pay more for fertiliser use less, harvests shrink, and food prices rise. The IMF estimates that almost half of a fertiliser price rise ends up in food prices, and Capital Economics expects the biggest effect around 15 months after the shock. That puts the peak in 2027, probably after the war ends. It will hit hardest where food is a large share of what people spend: sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.

## Nigeria: a windfall most Nigerians didn't feel

On paper, Nigeria should be one of the war's winners. Oil is most of what it sells abroad, and the 2026 budget assumed oil at just $64.85 a barrel, later raised to $75. Prices averaged about $97 over the first six months of the war.

**The windfall was smaller than it looked.** Nigeria produced about 1.6 million barrels a day, below the 1.84 million the budget assumed, because of theft, damaged pipelines and underinvestment. Some oil had also been sold in advance at fixed prices. One estimate puts the extra revenue at about $3.6 billion (₦4.8 trillion) over six months. And the Excess Crude Account, the savings pot meant for exactly this kind of windfall, held about $536,000 in June: unchanged since August 2025.

**The naira fell first, then recovered.** This was the strangest part. Oil rose more than 50% in the first weeks of the war, and the naira still *fell*, from ₦1,336 per dollar on 17 February to about ₦1,405 in early March. The reason was foreign "hot money": investors who had bought naira to earn Nigeria's high interest rates pulled out when the world got scared. That money moves in days. Oil money takes months to arrive. Once it did, the naira recovered, reaching about ₦1,333 in mid-October, its strongest in two years, with reserves around $55 billion.

**Petrol got much more expensive.** Since the subsidy ended, pump prices follow world prices. Dangote's refinery price rose from ₦699 a litre in December 2025 to ₦1,175 by March. Between late February and mid-March, Nigerian petrol prices rose about 40%, the steepest increase in Africa. On 3 November, Dangote moved to a fixed price of ₦1,300 a litre.

**But it could have been worse.** In past oil shocks, Nigeria imported almost all its petrol, so higher prices came with queues and shortages too. This time, much of the fuel was made at home, which is a big reason I wrote that the [Dangote refinery](/blog/what-the-dangote-refinery-means-for-nigeria.html) matters. Meanwhile, high refining profits during the war also flattered the refinery's results, which is part of why I thought its [IPO price](/blog/why-i-did-not-invest-in-dangotes-ipo.html) was too high.

**Inflation kept falling, for now.** Headline inflation fell to 14.63% in September, from 15.39% in August. But the fertiliser shock hasn't reached food prices yet.

**And one link I didn't expect:** Nigeria makes and exports urea, through plants like Dangote's in Lagos and Indorama's in Port Harcourt. The same fertiliser shock that threatens food prices in much of Africa could be earning Nigeria more dollars.

My take: the naira's fall in March is [Soros's reflexivity](/blog/the-alchemy-of-finance.html) in action. Fear makes investors sell naira, the naira falls, which makes the remaining investors more nervous, which makes them sell. Oil was supposed to protect the naira, and in the end it did. But it took months, and in the short run, the hot money mattered more than the oil.

## What I'm watching

- **Whether Hormuz reopens.** Saxo Bank expects oil to trade at $70–90 once the war ends, higher than the $60–70 before it. That would ease inflation, reduce the pressure to raise interest rates, and probably help gold.
- **What a reopening does to the naira.** Cheaper oil would mean fewer dollars coming in, but calmer markets could bring the hot money back. Which effect wins will say a lot about what really drives the naira now.
- **Food prices in 2027.** Even if the war ended tomorrow, the fertiliser shock is still on its way.
- **My calls.** Two of the three calls I made for 31 December in my midterms post were that the dollar recovers and gold stays between $3,900 and $4,050. Both are moving the right way so far, mostly because of this war's effect on inflation. Seven weeks is a long time, though.

## What I'm taking from it

The war looked like one event. In the markets, it was four different stories: a headline-driven rollercoaster in oil, a fall in gold that only makes sense through interest rates, a split in currencies by who pays for energy, and a windfall for Nigeria's government that ordinary Nigerians mostly felt as higher prices.

> In an oil shock, the first question isn't who's fighting. It's who buys the oil, who sells it, and how quickly the money moves.
