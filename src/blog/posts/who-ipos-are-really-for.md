Two share sales have taken up a lot of my attention over the past couple of months. The first is the Dangote refinery, which I wrote about in [why I didn't invest in Dangote's IPO](/blog/why-i-did-not-invest-in-dangotes-ipo.html). Its shares start trading in Lagos on 8 December. The second is Anthropic, the AI company behind Claude, which sets its IPO price tomorrow and starts trading on the Nasdaq on Thursday.

Both made me think about a rule I've been carrying around: **IPOs are for the company, not for everyday investors.** The way I saw it, the IPO price usually isn't fair value, and the ordinary person ends up paying more than they should so that the company and its early investors can benefit.

That's a strong claim, so before writing it down, I checked it. One half held up. The other half was the wrong way round.

## What an IPO actually is

An IPO (initial public offering) is the first time a private company sells its shares to the public.

The price isn't set by the market. It's set by the banks running the sale. They tour big investors, like pension funds and hedge funds, collect orders ("we'd buy this many shares at this price"), and use those orders to pick a price. Traders call this **book building**. Only after that do the shares start trading freely, and that's when everyone else can buy.

So there are really two prices: the **IPO price**, which a chosen group of buyers pays, and the **first trading price**, which everyone else pays.

## What I got wrong: the IPO price is usually too *low*

Jay Ritter, a finance professor at the University of Florida, has tracked over 9,000 US IPOs going back to 1980. On average, the shares closed their first day of trading **18.9% above the IPO price**.

If the IPO price were usually too high, you'd expect the opposite. Instead, companies typically sell their shares for less than the market will pay the next morning. People in the industry call this "leaving money on the table". The banks price low on purpose: it makes sure the whole sale gets bought, it rewards the big investors who gave them honest orders, and a strong first day makes the company look good.

So the first part of my rule was wrong. If you get shares *at the IPO price*, the odds have historically been in your favour, at least on day one.

## What I got right: everyday investors rarely get that price

The catch is who gets those shares.

For most of IPO history, individual investors received a tiny slice, often just 1% to 5% of the shares. Most went to large institutions. Even now, 5% to 10% is typical for large US IPOs.

It gets worse. When an IPO is popular, there are many more orders than shares, so everyone's order gets cut back. When an IPO is unpopular, the big investors drop out and you get every share you asked for. Economists call this the **winner's curse**: you get all the shares you want in exactly the deals you shouldn't want.

That leaves most everyday investors buying on the first day of trading, *after* the jump. And that's the price that tends to be too high. In Ritter's data, if you bought at the end of the first day and held for three years, you made 19% on average. That sounds fine, until you compare it with simply buying the whole market: IPOs **trailed the market by about 20%** over those three years. Even against similar-sized companies, they trailed by about 9%.

So my instinct was pointing at the right person, the ordinary investor, but at the wrong price. The IPO price isn't the problem. The problem is that most people never get it.

## SpaceX: the textbook example

The biggest IPO in history, SpaceX in June, followed the pattern almost exactly.

- **IPO price:** $135.
- **First day:** closed at $160.95, up 19.2%, almost exactly Ritter's average.
- **Peak:** about $226 within weeks.
- **By mid-July:** below $135 for the first time.
- **Early August:** a low of about $105.
- **Last week:** about $143.

What you made depended entirely on *when* you got in. If you got shares at the IPO price, you're up about 6%. If you bought at the first-day close, you're down about 11%. If you bought at the peak, you're down more than a third.

## Who an IPO is really for

Here's who benefits, in rough order:

1. **The company.** It raises cash it doesn't have to pay back. Dangote raised about ₦2.15 trillion. Anthropic's sale could raise around $64 billion, almost all of it for the company itself.
2. **Early investors.** An IPO lets them sell, often at a much higher price than they paid. In February, Anthropic raised money at a $380 billion valuation. In May, it was $965 billion. At the top of its IPO range, it's about $1.4 trillion. In Dangote's case, private investors paid about $0.35 a share in July, and the public paid about $0.40 three months later.
3. **The banks.** They earn fees for running the sale.
4. **Investors who get an IPO allocation.** Historically, they get the first-day jump.
5. **Everyone buying on the first day of trading.** Last in line, at the most excited price.

So "IPOs are for the company" is too simple. A more accurate version is: **IPOs are for the people already inside, and the people the banks let in first.**

## My two IPOs, side by side

**Dangote refinery**
- **Price:** ₦525 a share, valuing the refinery at about ₦65 trillion ($49 billion).
- **Public share:** about 3% of the company. Aliko Dangote keeps about 84%.
- **Demand:** about three times oversubscribed.
- **Trading starts:** 8 December, on the Nigerian Exchange.

**Anthropic**
- **Price:** $70 to $78 a share, for 825 million shares. At the top of the range, that values the company at about $1.4 trillion.
- **Public share:** up to 20% of the shares on offer are set aside for individual investors, in the US and other countries, including the UK through platforms like Hargreaves Lansdown, AJ Bell and Robinhood.
- **Demand:** reported to be about six times oversubscribed.
- **Trading starts:** Thursday 19 November, on the Nasdaq.

Anthropic's 20% is worth noting, because it pushes against my rule. It's two to four times what individuals normally get, and UK investors usually can't take part in US IPOs at all. That's a real change.

But six times oversubscribed means most people who apply will get a small allocation, or none. Most individuals who want Anthropic shares will still end up buying on Thursday, at whatever price the excitement sets.

## Where my rule needs refining

The data also showed me that not all IPOs are equal. Ritter splits them up:

- **Non-tech IPOs** trailed the market by about 25% over three years.
- **Tech IPOs** trailed it by about 13%, and performed roughly in line with similar-sized tech companies.

So the "IPO curse" is strongest in small, non-tech companies. Big, well-known tech names have done less badly. That doesn't make any single IPO a good buy. But my rule was too broad.

## What I'm taking from it

My old rule: IPOs are priced to benefit the company, so everyday investors overpay.

My new rule: **IPO prices are usually too low, for the people who get them. For everyone else, the first price you can actually pay is usually the most excited one.**

So in practice, I still won't chase an IPO on day one. If I'm interested in a new company, I'd rather wait until the excitement fades, until it has reported results as a listed company, and until the early investors are allowed to sell (usually around six months after listing). Then the question becomes the same one I ask about anything else: is the business worth more than the price?

And I'll test this, the same way I'm testing my Dangote call. In a year's time, I'll check both Dangote and Anthropic against two prices: the IPO price, and the close on their first day of trading. If my new rule is right, the first should look a lot better than the second.

This isn't advice. It's the rule I'm using for myself.

> The IPO price usually isn't the problem. The problem is that most people never get it.
