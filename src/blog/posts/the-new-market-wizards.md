The next book on my reading list is Jack Schwager's *The New Market Wizards*, from 1992. It's different from the last two. [A Random Walk Down Wall Street](/blog/a-random-walk-down-wall-street.html) and [The Little Book That Beats the Market](/blog/the-little-book-that-beats-the-market.html) each make one argument. This book is a series of interviews with traders who had beaten the market for years, each in their own way. There's no single thesis to agree or disagree with, so I've grouped the traders by style and picked out what I took from each.

## The big idea

There's no single way to win. The traders in this book use completely different methods: some follow strict computer rules, some trade on instinct, some bet big on one idea, some make thousands of tiny bets. But they share the same habits: strict risk control, discipline, patience, and a method that suits their personality.

## The FX traders

These were the chapters I read most closely, because currencies are where I want to work.

**Bill Lipschutz** was a star currency trader at Salomon Brothers. His key lesson is to watch how the market reacts to news, not just the news itself. If good news comes out and the price doesn't rise, something is wrong, and the market is telling you so. I saw a version of this last week, when the [Bank of England raised rates](/blog/bank-of-england-raises-rates.html) for the first time since 2023 and the pound barely moved. Lipschutz also learned the hard way never to let a position grow out of control.

**Randy McKay** traded currency futures for decades. When he's losing, he cuts his position size until he's back in rhythm. My take: it sounds almost too simple to be a lesson, but it's vital. A bad run with full-size positions can end a career. A bad run with small positions is just a bad month. Protecting your money during losing streaks is what keeps you in the game long enough for the good streaks.

## The systematic and trend traders

**William Eckhardt** co-ran the famous "Turtles" experiment, in which he and Richard Dennis trained a group of beginners to trade with a fixed set of rules, and many did very well. It showed trading can be taught. Eckhardt's sharpest point is that human instincts work against you: taking a profit early feels good, and is usually the wrong decision. Rules exist to stop you doing what feels natural.

**Monroe Trout** focused on steady returns with very low risk. For him, not losing big mattered more than winning big.

**Tom Basso** is calm and systematic, and puts process ahead of outcome. A good decision can lose money and a bad one can make money, so you judge the decision, not the result. That's the same lesson I drew from my [mean reversion project](/projects/fx-commodities-mean-reversion.html): a result is only worth trusting if the process behind it holds up.

## The big-picture and stock investors

**Stanley Druckenmiller** was George Soros's partner in the famous 1992 bet against the pound, when the Bank of England couldn't hold the currency up and Britain left Europe's exchange rate system. His lesson: when you have strong conviction, bet big. It isn't how often you're right that matters, but how much you make when you are, and how little you lose when you're not. My take: this is the classic macro FX story, and the chapter closest to the world of the person who gave me this reading list.

**Richard Driehaus** buys fast-growing companies whose shares are already rising. He's a useful counterpoint to Greenblatt, who buys good businesses when they're cheap. Driehaus shows that momentum, buying what's already going up, can work just as well as value. Malkiel admitted momentum was the one pattern that seemed to hold up, too.

**Gil Blake** started out believing markets were a random walk, then found genuine patterns in the data that he could trade. My take: he's a nice bridge to Malkiel. Markets are mostly efficient, but not completely, and the people who find the gaps are the ones who actually looked.

**Victor Sperandeo** argues that the core skill is accepting you're wrong and cutting your losses quickly. Everything else comes after that.

**Linda Bradford Raschke** is a short-term trader who reads the market's rhythm through constant practice. Her chapter is a reminder that some skills only come from screen time.

## The options and odds players

This group linked most directly to the kind of firms I'm interested in.

**Jeff Yass** founded Susquehanna (SIG), the firm behind the Algothon trading challenge I had a go at. He treats trading like poker: think in probabilities, and make the best decision you can with what you know, accepting that luck will decide individual hands. My take: this explains why trading firms test probability puzzles and games in interviews. They aren't checking whether you can do sums. They're checking how you think when you don't know the answer.

**Blair Hull** went from counting cards at blackjack to making markets in options. His lesson: find a small edge, then manage your money so you survive the bad runs long enough for the edge to show. It's the same idea behind modern market makers like XTX: a tiny edge, repeated many times, with enough money held back to survive the days it goes wrong.

**Joe Ritchie** mixes intuition with theory, a reminder that even in maths-heavy trading, judgement still matters.

## The psychology chapters

**Robert Krausz** and **Charles Faulkner** focus on the beliefs and mental habits of successful traders rather than their methods. Their point is that mindset isn't fixed: you can train it, the same way you train a skill.

## The final lessons

Schwager pulls it all together at the end. Find a method that suits you. Manage risk above everything else. Be disciplined. Be patient. And accept losses as part of the job, not as proof you've failed.

## How it fits with the other two

Reading the three books in a row, they fit together better than I expected.

- **Malkiel** says markets are very hard to beat.
- **Greenblatt** says patience can be an edge, because most people can't stick with a strategy through its bad years.
- **Schwager** shows the people who did beat the market. Each found their own edge, and every one of them survived long enough to use it.

That last point is the one that matters. None of these traders won because they were right every time. They won because their losing periods never knocked them out.

## What I'm taking from it

Before reading this, I thought the hard part of trading was finding the right strategy. Now I think the strategy is the part that varies most from person to person. What doesn't vary is the discipline around it.

> The common thread wasn't the strategy. It was risk management and discipline. That's what I'm trying to build first.
