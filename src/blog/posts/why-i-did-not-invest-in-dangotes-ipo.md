Nigeria has just had the biggest share sale in its history. Dangote Petroleum Refinery offered 4.1 billion new shares at ₦525 each, raising about ₦2.15 trillion, roughly $1.6 billion. Investors asked for about ₦6.4 trillion, three times what was on offer. The shares are due to start trading on the Nigerian Exchange this month.

I believe in what this refinery could do for Nigeria. I still didn't buy a single share. This post is about why, and it's my own reasoning, not advice to anyone else.

## Why the refinery matters

For decades, Nigeria has pumped crude oil out of the ground, shipped it abroad, and then imported the petrol made from it, paying for the round trip in dollars. Dangote's refinery in Lagos, built to process 650,000 barrels a day, is designed to end that. By the middle of this year it was supplying most of Nigeria's petrol, and its production costs were falling fast.

As someone who believes in what Nigeria can become, I think this could be one of the first big steps towards it. Making things at home instead of importing them is how countries build industry, jobs and a stronger currency. I want this refinery to succeed.

But wanting a company to succeed and wanting to own its shares at a particular price are two different decisions. That's the main thing my reading list has taught me so far.

## Reason 1: the price was too high for what it offered

At ₦525 a share, the whole company was valued at about ₦65 trillion, roughly $49 billion.

The question I kept asking was what I'd be paying for. In the first half of 2026 the refinery made about $1.8 billion of profit. That's a strong number, but it came during the Iran war, when disruption to oil shipping pushed refining profits to unusual highs. If you double it for a full year, you get an earnings yield of about 7%: about $3.6 billion of profit for a $49 billion company. That's not much, given everything that could go wrong, and it assumes war-time profits last.

They might not. On 4 November, Brent oil fell almost 9% in a day as traders bet on peace, as I wrote in my [post on the US midterms](/blog/what-the-midterms-did-to-the-dollar.html). If refining profits return to normal, the price looks very stretched. One analyst who modelled exactly that put fair value at about ₦207 a share, around 60% below the offer price.

This is where Greenblatt's idea of a [margin of safety](/blog/the-little-book-that-beats-the-market.html) comes in. You want to buy well below what something is worth, so that being wrong doesn't hurt much. At ₦525, I couldn't see any margin of safety at all. If anything, the price seemed to need everything to go right.

## Reason 2: IPOs tend to favour the people selling, not the people buying

My second reason is about IPOs in general. The way I see it, the offer price is set for the benefit of the company and the people who got in early, more than for everyday investors buying in at the end.

The pattern I've noticed is that excitement peaks around the listing, and new shares often struggle to get back to that level for years afterwards. Research on thousands of IPOs backs up part of this. On average, new shares often jump on their first day, then lag the wider market over the next few years. So if you buy at the offer price and get your full allocation, you might do well on day one. But if you want to hold for the long term, waiting until the excitement has faded has often been the better entry.

Dangote's IPO showed a few of the signs that worry me:

- **The early money paid less.** Private investors bought shares in July at about $0.35 each. The public paid about $0.40 three months later, around 13% more.
- **We get a very small slice, and almost no say.** The public offer was only about 3% of the company. Aliko Dangote keeps about 84%, so outside shareholders have very little influence over how it's run.
- **Demand isn't the same as value.** Part of the rush came from national pride, and from Nigerian investors with few other large companies to buy. [Malkiel](/blog/a-random-walk-down-wall-street.html) would call that a castle in the air: a price set by how keen the next buyer is, not by what the business earns.

To be fair, these were new shares, so the money raised goes to the refinery itself, not into anyone's pocket. That's better than an IPO where founders are just cashing out. But the price was still set to suit the company, and the public got the least favourable terms of anyone.

## Reason 3: a refinery this big changes the whole industry

This is the reason that's most personal to me.

My first job was at an oil and gas company working across Africa, with Nigeria as its main market. So I can't look at this refinery only as a share. I also see what it means for every other company in the industry.

A refinery supplying most of a country's petrol is close to a monopoly, and monopolies cut both ways:

- **The good side:** reliable local fuel, fewer imports, less pressure on the naira, and one big buyer for Nigerian crude right on the doorstep.
- **The harder side:** fuel importers and smaller refineries can be squeezed out. And when one company is the main buyer of crude in a country, it has huge power over the price oil producers get. Economists call that a monopsony: the buyer's version of a monopoly.

A business with that much power over a market will always end up in disputes with regulators, rivals and the government. That's a risk for its shareholders, and it's a big reason I wouldn't feel comfortable owning it.

## Would I buy at a lower price?

At somewhere around ₦150 to ₦200 a share, it would look much more reasonable on the numbers, and that's close to the analyst estimate above.

But honestly, I don't think I'd buy even then. With one owner holding 84% and the company sitting at the centre of so many conflicts across Nigeria's oil industry, I'd be a tiny shareholder with no say in decisions that will be shaped by politics as much as by business. I'd rather watch it from the outside, cheer it on, and learn from how it plays out.

So my call isn't "buy below ₦X". It's a test of my second reason. I'll look back in a year and see whether shares bought at the ₦525 offer price have actually rewarded long-term holders, or whether, like many IPOs before it, the price peaked around the listing.

## What I'm taking from it

This was the first time I've had to separate what I believe in from what I'd invest in. I believe in this refinery, and I believe in Nigeria. But a good company at the wrong price, with the wrong terms, is still a bad investment for me.

> Supporting a company and owning its shares are two different decisions. I can do the first without the second.
