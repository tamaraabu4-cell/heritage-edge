On 30 May 2026, in Budapest, Gabriel stepped up to take Arsenal's fifth penalty in the Champions League final. He put it over the bar, and Paris Saint-Germain won the shootout 4–3.

A week earlier, the same team had won the Premier League for the first time since the Invincibles. Same players, same manager, same method. One competition ended in a trophy, the other in heartbreak.

Over the last few weeks I've read three books from my reading list: [A Random Walk Down Wall Street](/blog/a-random-walk-down-wall-street.html), [The Little Book That Beats the Market](/blog/the-little-book-that-beats-the-market.html) and [The New Market Wizards](/blog/the-new-market-wizards.html). They kept coming back to the same few ideas: look where others aren't looking, stick with your method through the bad patches, and above all, don't lose big. Then I watched a documentary on Alex Gerko, the founder of XTX Markets, one of the biggest trading firms in the world, and realised the clearest example of all three wasn't in a book. It was my football club.

## Two careers that look alike

Arteta was a good Arsenal player, and later captain, but never the star of the team. He learned coaching as Pep Guardiola's assistant at Manchester City. Then, in December 2019, he took his first job as a manager, at a club sitting 11th in the league.

Gerko was a quant at Deutsche Bank, building trading models rather than being the famous trader on the desk. He learned to run a team inside a London hedge fund, GSA Capital, on computers and money that belonged to someone else. In 2015 he left to start XTX, his first firm of his own.

Neither was the obvious star. Both built something nobody expected, by doing it differently.

## The lean years

Arteta's start was rough. Arsenal won the FA Cup in his first season but finished 8th in the league, then 8th again: their lowest finishes since the mid-1990s. His line about "trusting the process" became a joke among rival fans. After a game where Arsenal put in 33 crosses, he called it "maths, pure maths", and Arsenal lost three of their next four.

But the club kept faith in the method, not the results.

XTX's early years had the same shape. By Gerko's own account, his old firm even switched off his team's trading systems in the middle of negotiations over his exit. He went independent weeks after the Swiss franc crash in January 2015, a terrible moment to start a trading firm. In the first industry rankings after it launched, XTX was only ninth in currency trading overall.

This is the idea from Greenblatt's book that stuck with me most: some edges survive not because people can't see them, but because they can't stick with them. His formula lags the market for two or three years at a time, and that's exactly when most people give up. Two 8th-place finishes is a football club's version of that drought. Most boards would have sacked the manager. Arsenal's didn't, and that patience was part of the edge.

## Built on defence

Most of Arsenal's rivals are built to outscore you. Arteta's Arsenal are built so you can't score against them. They won the league conceding just 27 goals, and David Raya kept 19 clean sheets.

That's the heart of what XTX does too. It's a market maker: it's always ready to buy or sell, and it earns the small gap between the two prices, like an airport currency booth. Its biggest danger is the customer who knows more. If someone knows a price is about to fall and sells to you, you lose. Traders call it adverse selection. In football terms, it's getting caught on the counter-attack.

XTX's answer is a forecast of where prices will go in the next few minutes. When it sees a fall coming, it quietly offers worse prices, so it can't be picked off. That's defence.

And good defence lets you attack. Because XTX fears the better-informed customer less, it can offer everyone else sharper prices and win more trades. A team that rarely concedes can afford to push more players forward.

The New Market Wizards made the same point about people. The traders in it used completely different methods, but the one thing they all shared was risk control. Randy McKay cuts his position size when he's losing. Victor Sperandeo says the core skill is cutting losses quickly. In trading, defence has a name: risk management. And it's what keeps you in the game long enough for your attack to matter.

## The edge everyone else overlooked

While most teams treated corners as a break in play, Arteta treated them as a chance to score. He says the plan goes back a decade, to his time at City. Arsenal have scored 37 league goals from corners since 2023–24, 16 more than any other club.

XTX found its own overlooked edge. Most high-speed trading firms race to be the *fastest*, spending fortunes to see prices a sliver of a second sooner. XTX tried to be the most *right* instead, using machine learning to predict prices better rather than faster. It also stepped in just as the big banks were pulling back after the 2008 crisis. By 2019, it was the biggest electronic spot currency dealer in the world, ahead of JPMorgan.

This is exactly where Malkiel says an edge can still live. In the crowded places, where everyone is competing on the same thing, there's almost nothing left. The edge is in what everyone else is walking past: a set piece most teams treated as admin, or a way of competing most trading firms weren't even trying.

## Second again

Arsenal led the league for 248 days in 2022–23 and still finished second. Then second again. And again. Rival fans sang "Second again" at us in summer 2025.

XTX had its own setback. After it took top spot, JPMorgan poured money into its own trading machines, and by 2021 XTX had slipped to fourth.

That's the uncomfortable part of any edge: if it works, people copy it. Every club now has a set-piece coach. Every bank now has trading algorithms. Malkiel would say the edge got priced in. So the edge isn't a trick you find once. It's the habit of looking where others don't, and continuing to build when you're not winning yet.

## The league and the final

This is where last season taught me the most.

A league title is 38 games. Over that many matches, luck mostly evens out, and the best method usually wins. A penalty shootout is close to the opposite: five kicks each, decided by small margins and nerve. Arsenal won every game in the Champions League's league phase, finishing ten points ahead of PSG, and still lost the final from the spot.

Trading works the same way. XTX doesn't make money from one big bet. It makes a tiny edge on each trade, across more than $250 billion of trading a day. Any single trade can lose. Over a whole "season" of trades, the edge shows. Blair Hull, the blackjack card counter turned options market maker in The New Market Wizards, built his career on the same idea: find a small edge, repeat it many times, and keep enough back to survive the bad runs.

Jeff Yass, who founded Susquehanna, describes trading as poker: you make the best decision you can, and accept that luck decides individual hands. Tom Basso puts it more simply: judge the process, not the outcome. That's the lesson I took from my own [mean reversion project](/projects/fx-commodities-mean-reversion.html) too. A good trade can lose money, and a good team can lose a final.

## Where the comparison breaks

It isn't perfect. A match has a final whistle and a market doesn't. In football you know who you're playing; in trading you often don't. And in markets, the rules can change mid-game.

## What I'm taking from it

Each book on my reading list taught me one thing, and Arteta and Gerko show all three:

- **Malkiel:** look where others aren't looking. Corners, and prediction over speed.
- **Greenblatt:** stick with the method through the bad years. Two 8th places, and ninth in the rankings.
- **Schwager:** above all, don't lose big. 27 goals conceded, and a forecast that stops XTX getting picked off.

Neither of them won by being the most exciting. They won by being hard to beat.

> Most people think trading, like football, is won by attack. The best teams in both are built the other way round.
