The last book on my reading list is *The Alchemy of Finance* by George Soros, from 1987. It's the most theoretical of the five, and the hardest to read. Later editions add new material, so the chapter numbers vary between copies. I've grouped it by its five parts instead.

## The big idea

Most of finance assumes markets work like a set of scales: prices move towards the "correct" value, and when they get it wrong, they correct. Soros says that's not how it works.

People always act on beliefs that are partly wrong. And in markets, acting on those beliefs changes the real world, which then changes the beliefs. He calls this two-way loop **reflexivity**. It's why booms and busts feed on themselves, and why he thinks a trader's real job is to spot those loops early.

## Part One: the theory (roughly chapters 1–4)

A weather forecast doesn't change the weather. But in markets, what people believe changes the thing they're judging.

Soros's example is the conglomerate boom of the 1960s. Investors believed certain companies were great, so their share prices soared. Those companies then used their expensive shares to buy other companies cheaply, which made their profits grow, which seemed to prove the investors right, which pushed the shares even higher. The belief made itself true, for a while.

Every boom like this follows the same shape:

1. A trend starts.
2. Belief reinforces it.
3. It overshoots what the real world can support.
4. It gets tested.
5. Once the gap between belief and reality gets too wide, it reverses, often violently.

He applies the same idea to currencies. When exchange rates float freely, speculators following a trend push a currency further. That changes trade, inflation and where money flows, which reinforces the trend. So currencies don't settle at a calm "fair value". They swing in long cycles that feed themselves.

And to credit: lending pushes up the value of the things borrowed against, like property, which makes the borrowers look safer, which justifies more lending. Until it doesn't.

My take: this is a direct challenge to [Malkiel](/blog/a-random-walk-down-wall-street.html). Malkiel says prices reflect reality. Soros says prices help *create* reality. I think both are partly right. Most days, Malkiel describes the market well. But in a boom or a crisis, Soros does, and knowing which world you're in is an edge in itself.

## Part Two: history (roughly chapters 5–9)

Soros then uses the theory to explain the big events of his time.

**The 1980s debt crisis.** Banks lent heavily to Latin American countries. The loans looked safe because the lending kept going: new money paid the interest on the old money. When the lending stopped in 1982, starting with Mexico, the whole thing cracked.

**Reagan's "imperial circle".** In the early 1980s, the US ran big budget deficits with high interest rates. High rates pulled in money from abroad, which made the dollar stronger, and that foreign money paid for the deficits. Each part held up the others. It looked stable, but it depended on foreign money continuing to flow in, so it couldn't last.

My take: the imperial circle is the clearest FX example in the book. A strong currency, high rates and foreign money all propping each other up looks like strength, right up until one piece moves.

## Part Three: the real-time experiment (roughly chapters 10–15)

This was the part I found most useful.

From 1985 to 1986, Soros kept a diary of his fund's real trades, writing down his reasoning *before* he knew how each trade would turn out. You get to watch one of the best macro traders in history think, guess, get things wrong and change his mind, all in real time.

The highlight is his bet that the dollar would fall against the Japanese yen and the German mark. In September 1985, the governments of five major economies signed the Plaza Accord, agreeing to push the dollar down together. Soros was already positioned for it, and the trade made a fortune.

My take: the lesson isn't that he was right. It's how he worked: form a view, bet on it, test it constantly, and drop it fast when the market says you're wrong. Writing your reasoning down *before* the result is what makes that honest, because you can't rewrite what you thought afterwards. It's why I put my calls in writing in my [midterms post](/blog/what-the-midterms-did-to-the-dollar.html), and why I said in my [Dangote post](/blog/why-i-did-not-invest-in-dangotes-ipo.html) that I'll look back in a year and score myself.

## Part Four: the verdict (roughly chapter 16 onwards)

Looking back on the experiment, Soros judges his own theory. It helped him spot turning points. But his results depended just as much on admitting his mistakes quickly.

That's the same conclusion as every other book on the list, arrived at from a completely different direction.

## Part Five: what he'd change

Soros argues that markets left completely alone can be unstable, because the loops can run too far. So they need sensible rules and countries working together. Later editions also cover the 1987 crash, and some reflect on his most famous trade: the 1992 bet against the pound, which I first read about through [Druckenmiller in The New Market Wizards](/blog/the-new-market-wizards.html).

## Why it matters for FX, and for Nigeria

Reflexivity explains currency crises better than anything else I've read.

Take an emerging market currency like the naira. The currency falls. Imports get more expensive, so inflation rises. Foreign investors see their returns shrinking and pull their money out, which means selling naira, which pushes it down further. Each step feeds the next. Nobody has to be irrational for this to happen. Everyone is responding sensibly to what everyone else is doing.

The loop can run the other way too. In my [refinery post](/blog/what-the-dangote-refinery-means-for-nigeria.html), I wrote that making fuel at home means fewer dollars leaving the country. Less pressure on the naira means lower inflation, which makes Nigeria more attractive to foreign money, which supports the naira. If that loop takes hold, it's worth more than the refinery's own profits.

My take: in my [Reminiscences post](/blog/reminiscences-of-a-stock-operator.html), I wrote about the skill I've been told to build: seeing links others can't. Reflexivity is a framework for exactly that. Instead of asking "what happens next?", Soros asks "how does this belief change reality, and how does that change the belief?"

## How it fits with the other books

This is the last book on the list, so here's all five in one line each:

- **[Malkiel](/blog/a-random-walk-down-wall-street.html):** markets are very hard to beat, so look where others aren't.
- **[Greenblatt](/blog/the-little-book-that-beats-the-market.html):** patience is an edge, because most people can't stick with a strategy.
- **[Schwager](/blog/the-new-market-wizards.html):** there are many ways to win, but only one way to survive: managing risk.
- **[Livermore](/blog/reminiscences-of-a-stock-operator.html):** the tools change, but the mistakes don't, because people don't.
- **Soros:** prices don't just reflect the world, they change it, and the biggest moves come from those loops.

Soros fills the gap in the other four. Malkiel explains why the market is usually right. Livermore explains that trends exist. Soros explains *why* trends exist: beliefs and reality feeding each other, until they can't any more. And like every other trader on the list, his edge came down to admitting mistakes quickly.

## What I'm taking from it

This book changed how I look at FX. I used to think of a currency's price as an answer: the market's best guess at what it's worth. Now I think of it as part of the question, because the price itself changes trade, inflation and money flows.

> Soros gave me a different lens on FX. Prices don't just reflect the fundamentals, they change them. That's why emerging market currencies can spiral, and why I want to understand those loops, especially in markets like Nigeria.
