The next book on my reading list is the oldest so far: *Reminiscences of a Stock Operator* by Edwin Lefèvre, published in 1923. It's a lightly fictionalised life of Jesse Livermore, one of the most famous speculators in history, told through a character called Larry Livingston. It reads more like a novel than a textbook: 24 short chapters that flow as one story. So instead of going chapter by chapter, I've split it into the stages of his career.

## The big idea

Markets never really change, because people don't. Fear, hope, greed and ignorance moved prices in 1907 exactly the way they move them today. The tools are completely different. The emotions are the same. And the trader's real battle isn't with the market, it's with themselves.

## Chapters 1–4: the boy wonder, and his first crash

As a teenager, Livingston gets a job chalking up share prices on a board in a broker's office. Watching the numbers all day, he starts noticing patterns in how they move. He takes that skill into **bucket shops**: places where you bet on price moves, but your order never actually reaches the real market. You're simply betting against the shop. He's so good at it that they ban him.

Then he moves to a real brokerage in New York, and goes broke. In a bucket shop you trade at the price on the board. In a real market, by the time your order arrives, the price has moved, and you don't get what you saw.

My take: bucket shops are surprisingly close to parts of today's retail trading world. Some retail brokers take the other side of their clients' trades instead of sending them to the market, and prop firm challenges are traded on simulated accounts. The lesson is the same as Livingston's: a strategy that works in a simulated environment can fail in a real one. It's an idea I keep coming back to in my own notes: *where* and *how* your order actually fills is part of the trade.

## Chapters 5–8: the big lesson, and the big win

There's an old trader in the office called Partridge. Whenever an eager young trader asks him for advice on a stock, he just reminds them that it's a bull market. It takes Livingston years to understand him. The big money isn't made by catching every small up and down. It's made by recognising the main trend and sitting tight while it plays out.

Livingston puts this into practice spectacularly. He bets against the market before the Panic of 1907 and makes a fortune in a single day. Then he's asked, on behalf of J.P. Morgan, to stop selling to help save the market, and he does.

My take: this is two lessons from my last two books in one story. It's [Greenblatt's patience](/blog/the-little-book-that-beats-the-market.html): the hard part isn't knowing what to do, it's sitting still while you wait. And it's Druckenmiller in [The New Market Wizards](/blog/the-new-market-wizards.html): when you're right and you know it, bet big.

## Chapters 9–12: ruined by someone else's opinion

This is the part that stuck with me most.

Livingston gets to know a persuasive cotton expert. Against his own judgement, he starts following the man's views. When the trade goes against him, he does the worst thing possible: he keeps buying more. Traders call this **averaging down**: buying more of something that's falling, just to lower your average price. He goes broke again.

My take: this is the most important warning in the book, and it's three lessons in one. Never trade on tips. Never let someone talk you out of your own analysis. And never add to a losing trade. All three are things that *feel* sensible in the moment, which is exactly what makes them dangerous.

## Chapters 13–16: the comeback

He rebuilds slowly, with a much stricter process. He waits for clear opportunities instead of trading all the time. He trades in the direction the market already "wants" to go, which he calls the **line of least resistance**. And he only adds to trades that are already working, never to ones that are losing.

That idea of trends having momentum lines up with something I found in my own [mean reversion project](/projects/fx-commodities-mean-reversion.html). I tested betting that big daily moves in currencies and commodities would snap back. They didn't. Big moves tended to keep going, because they were usually driven by real news. Livingston would not have been surprised.

Then a big coffee trade is wiped out, not because he was wrong, but because the government changed the rules in the middle of it.

My take: this links straight to Nigeria. The central bank there has changed foreign exchange rules overnight more than once, including limits on how much currency banks can hold. When the rules can change mid-game, regulatory risk is real risk, and it belongs in every trade plan. It's one of the reasons I wrote in my [Dangote IPO post](/blog/why-i-did-not-invest-in-dangotes-ipo.html) that a company so tied up in politics was hard for me to own.

## Chapters 17–20: hunches and human nature

Livingston thinks a lot about hunches: the times he sold before he could explain why, and turned out to be right. He decides they're not magic. They're his mind spotting patterns from years of watching prices, faster than he can put into words.

He also explains the two emotions that ruin traders. Hope keeps you in a losing trade, waiting for it to come back. Fear pushes you out of a winning trade too early, worried you'll lose what you've made. Both feel natural. Both are backwards.

My take: this is the skill the person who gave me this reading list told me to build, seeing links others can't, described from the inside. It isn't a gift. It's built through years of exposure to patterns, which is a big part of why I'm building my knowledge hub and trading process now.

## Chapters 21–24: how the insiders played

The last chapters describe how big operators of the time moved share prices: quietly buying or selling huge amounts, spreading tips, and luring the public in. Almost every time, the public trading on rumours ended up on the losing side.

My take: much of what's described here is illegal today. But the core lesson still stands. Large flows move prices, and the crowd acting on rumours tends to be last in and last out.

## A sober footnote

Livermore made and lost several fortunes in real life, and died in difficult circumstances in 1940. That changes how I read the book. Its lessons on risk aren't theory. He learned them, and paid for them, over and over again.

## How it fits with the other books

- **[Malkiel](/blog/a-random-walk-down-wall-street.html)** says short-term prices are mostly random.
- **Livermore** says prices move in trends driven by human nature.
- **The New Market Wizards** shows his rules still hold, seventy years later: cut losses, let winners run, stay disciplined.

I don't think Malkiel and Livermore are as far apart as they sound. Day to day, prices are mostly noise. But momentum, the one pattern Malkiel admitted keeps showing up, is exactly the trend Livermore spent his life riding.

## What I'm taking from it

This book is over a hundred years old, and it read like it was written about traders today. That's the strongest argument I've seen that the hardest part of trading isn't technical. It's personal.

> What stayed with me is that he went broke following other people's opinions, and made fortunes trusting his own analysis and waiting patiently. That's why I'm building my own process rather than following gurus.
