In my [last post](/blog/why-i-did-not-invest-in-dangotes-ipo.html) I explained why I didn't buy shares in the Dangote refinery. But I said I believe in what it's trying to do, and that deserves its own post. So this one is about the refinery itself: the problem it was built to solve, what it's already changed, and where it could still let Nigeria down.

## The strange problem it was built to solve

Nigeria is one of the biggest oil producers in Africa. Oil makes up most of what the country sells abroad. And yet, for most of my life, Nigeria has imported almost all of its petrol.

Think of a farmer who grows wheat, sells it all abroad, then buys bread back from overseas at a much higher price, paid for in a foreign currency. That's roughly what Nigeria has been doing with oil. Crude went out, and petrol, diesel and jet fuel came back in, all paid for in dollars. Every litre of imported fuel meant dollars leaving the country and pressure on the naira.

The country did have refineries of its own. The government-run plants in Port Harcourt, Warri and Kaduna were meant to supply the home market. For years they ran far below capacity or not at all. Nearly $3 billion was approved for repairs in 2021 alone, and as of last month, reports say none of the three is working. Dangote himself has said they may never work again.

## The subsidy years

To keep fuel affordable, the government spent decades paying the difference between what imported petrol cost and what Nigerians paid at the pump. That subsidy cost trillions of naira, money that couldn't go to schools, hospitals or roads, and it created space for smuggling and fraud.

In May 2023, the subsidy was removed. Pump prices rose sharply, from under ₦200 a litre to over ₦1,000 at times in 2024, and with the naira also falling, everyday life got much more expensive. Whatever you think of the decision, it changed the economics of fuel in Nigeria completely. For the first time in years, refining petrol at home and selling it at market prices could actually make money.

## Enter Dangote

Aliko Dangote, one of Africa's richest people, started building his refinery in Lekki, just outside Lagos, in 2016. It cost around $19 billion, began producing diesel and jet fuel in January 2024, and petrol from September 2024. It's built to process about 650,000 barrels a day, which makes it the biggest refinery in Africa and one of the biggest single-site refineries in the world.

The change has been fast. By January this year, it was supplying more than half of Nigeria's fuel, and over 60% of its petrol, overtaking importers for the first time. A series of price cuts helped bring pump prices back below ₦1,000 a litre.

## How it helps Nigeria

**1. Fewer dollars leaving the country.** Every litre made in Lagos is a litre that doesn't have to be bought abroad in dollars. Estimates put the potential saving at up to $10 billion a year. For a country whose currency has been under constant pressure, that's huge. In my [midterms post](/blog/what-the-midterms-did-to-the-dollar.html) I wrote that oil matters more to the naira than almost anything else. This refinery changes how oil affects it: less about how many dollars come in from crude, more about how few go out for fuel.

**2. Reliable supply.** Fuel queues and shortages have been a normal part of Nigerian life. A large refinery at home makes supply less dependent on ships, ports and foreign suppliers.

**3. Exports and regional power.** The refinery already sells fuel to neighbours including Ghana, Cameroon and Togo. Nigeria could become the fuel supplier for West Africa instead of a customer, earning dollars rather than spending them.

**4. Industry beyond fuel.** Refineries feed other industries: plastics, chemicals and fertiliser. A country that makes these at home can build factories and jobs around them. That's how industrial economies grow.

**5. Proof it can be done.** Maybe the most important thing. For decades, big infrastructure in Nigeria was something people expected to fail. A world-class plant, built and run in Nigeria, changes what investors and Nigerians themselves think is possible.

## Where it might not help

I'm hopeful, but I don't think one refinery fixes everything. There are real risks.

**1. Cheaper doesn't mean cheap.** The refinery sells at roughly the price imported fuel would cost. That's how it makes money. So when world oil prices rise, or the naira falls, Nigerians still pay more at the pump. It changes who supplies the fuel, not the fact that fuel is priced in dollars.

**2. Too much depends on one plant.** When one refinery supplies most of a country's petrol, any breakdown, fire, strike or dispute becomes a national problem. Nigeria swapped dependence on imports for dependence on a single site and a single owner.

**3. Too much power in one place.** As I said in my last post, a refinery this dominant is close to a monopoly in selling fuel, and the main buyer of Nigeria's crude, which gives it power over oil producers too. That brings constant fights with regulators, the national oil company and fuel marketers. Those fights have already happened, over import licences and over crude supply.

**4. It still struggles to buy Nigerian crude.** This is the part that surprised me most. One of Africa's biggest oil producers can't always supply its own biggest refinery. Theft, pipeline damage and supply disputes have meant Dangote has had to import crude from abroad, including from the US. Until Nigeria fixes its oil production, the refinery can't fully do what it was built for.

**5. It doesn't fix the public sector.** The state refineries failed after billions were spent on them. A private company succeeding doesn't solve whatever went wrong there. It just works around it.

**6. It deepens the bet on oil.** Nigeria has spent decades trying to depend less on oil. A giant refinery makes the country even more tied to it, at a time when the world is slowly moving away from fossil fuels.

## My view

My first job was at an oil and gas company working across Africa, with Nigeria as its main market. So I've seen a little of how hard it is to get things done in this industry, and how much Nigeria has lost by exporting crude and importing fuel for so long.

That's why I think this refinery matters. Not because it solves Nigeria's problems, but because it fixes one of the most obvious ones, and shows what Nigerian industry can look like. The risks are real, and most of them come down to one thing: too much of the country's fuel now depends on one company. The best outcome would be for Dangote to succeed and for competitors to grow up around it.

> The refinery fixes Nigeria's strangest problem: exporting crude and importing fuel. It doesn't fix the reasons that problem lasted so long.
