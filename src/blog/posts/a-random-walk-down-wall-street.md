Over coffee a few weeks ago, a former head of FX at a global bank gave me a reading list. The first book on it was Burton Malkiel's *A Random Walk Down Wall Street*, first published in 1973 and still updated today. I expected a book about trading. It's closer to a book about why most trading doesn't work.

## The big idea

In big, busy markets, prices already reflect most of what's known. New information arrives at random, so short-term price moves are close to random too: a "random walk". That means most people, professionals included, can't beat the market consistently once costs are counted. For most investors, Malkiel's answer is cheap, diversified index funds held for the long term.

That's an uncomfortable message for someone who wants to be a trader. Here's how the book builds the case, and where I ended up.

## Part one: what is anything worth?

Malkiel starts with two ways to value an asset:

- **Firm foundation:** it's worth the cash it will produce. A business is worth its future profits; a bond is worth its payments.
- **Castle in the air:** it's worth whatever the next buyer will pay for it.

Most of the time the two agree closely enough. Bubbles are when they come apart. The book walks through them in order: tulips in the 1630s, the South Sea Bubble, Japan in the 1980s, the dot-com boom, the 2008 housing crash and crypto. Every one came with a convincing story about why this time was different.

The lesson I took from these chapters isn't "bubbles are obvious". It's that **being right isn't enough**. Plenty of people correctly called the dot-com bubble a year or two early and lost money anyway. Timing, and surviving long enough to be proven right, matter as much as the view.

## Part two: how the professionals play

Next come the two main schools of stock picking:

- **Technical analysis** reads charts, looking for patterns that repeat.
- **Fundamental analysis** studies the business and estimates what it's really worth.

Malkiel is hard on charts. His evidence is that most chart patterns don't predict future prices once trading costs are included. That landed close to home: I started out learning retail chart methods, and it's part of why I've moved away from them. The important exception is **momentum**: prices that have been rising do tend to keep rising for a while, and that effect shows up in many markets. The difference is that firms trading it use statistically tested rules, not patterns spotted by eye.

He isn't much kinder to fundamental analysts. Even professionals struggle to beat the market after fees, which leads him to the **efficient market hypothesis**: prices already reflect available information, so it's very hard to find mispricings that are still there after costs.

I don't think "markets are efficient" is quite the right answer, though. The view I'd give in an interview is: **big, liquid markets are very efficient; smaller, overlooked ones are much less so.** The more people looking at a price, the less likely anything is left on the table.

I've since seen this in my own work. In my [mean reversion project](/projects/fx-commodities-mean-reversion.html), I tested whether EURUSD, USDJPY, gold and oil tend to undo yesterday's move. None of the four showed an edge that survived trading costs, a one-day delay in trading and a test on years the strategy had never seen. Malkiel would not have been surprised. What did survive was the gap between two related markets, Brent and WTI oil, which is a different and less crowded question than "where will oil go tomorrow?".

## Part three: the new investment technology

This part is a toolkit, and the definitions are worth knowing cold:

- **Diversification** is the one free lunch in finance. Combining assets that don't move together lowers risk without giving up return.
- **Beta** measures how much an asset moves with the market. A beta of 1.5 means it tends to move 1.5% when the market moves 1%.
- **CAPM** (the capital asset pricing model) says you're only paid for risk you can't diversify away, and beta measures that risk.
- **Behavioural finance** explains why people make predictable mistakes: overconfidence after a few wins, herding with the crowd, and loss aversion, where a loss hurts about twice as much as an equal gain feels good. The defence is writing down the plan for a trade before placing it, so the decision can be checked against it afterwards.

The chapters on **anomalies and smart beta** are where I disagree with Malkiel most. He's sceptical that factors like value and momentum offer a lasting edge once they're widely known. But real funds build whole strategies on them, including in currencies, where momentum and carry are standard. I'd say the edges are real but smaller, and more crowded, than they look in academic papers. That's still not the same as "there's nothing there".

## Part four: the practical guide

The last chapters are personal finance: estimating long-term returns, how much risk to take at different stages of life, and a strong case for index funds. It's sensible advice, and it's worth knowing that plenty of professional traders invest their own savings exactly this way. Trading is the job; index funds are where the savings go.

## What I'm taking from it

The same person who gave me the book also told me that the best traders see links other people miss. At first that sounds like the opposite of Malkiel. If markets are efficient, what is there to see?

I think both are true. Malkiel is describing the big, crowded markets, where thousands of people are looking at the same prices. The links others miss live in the places fewer people are looking: smaller currencies, including the African currencies I care about most, or the kind of overlooked assets most investors walk straight past.

So the book didn't change *whether* I want to find an edge. It changed where I'll look for one:

> Malkiel convinced me most markets are efficient most of the time, so my edge has to come from places that aren't.
