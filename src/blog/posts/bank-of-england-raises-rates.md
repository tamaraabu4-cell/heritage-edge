On Thursday, the Bank of England raised interest rates for the first time since August 2023, from 3.75% to 4%.

I wasn't surprised. Energy prices have been climbing for months, and a rise felt like a matter of time. What surprised me was the market's reaction, or rather the lack of one. Something this big should move prices, I thought. It barely did. This post is about why.

## What happened

- **The rise:** Bank Rate went up by 0.25 percentage points, to 4%.
- **The vote:** 8–1. Eight members voted for 0.25 points. One, Catherine Mann, wanted a bigger rise of 0.5 points.
- **The reason:** inflation rose to 3.5% in September, mainly because of higher fuel prices linked to the Middle East conflict. The Bank expects it to reach about 5% by mid-2027.
- **The message:** Governor Andrew Bailey said the rise "shouldn't be seen as the first in an inevitable long sequence of hikes", but he didn't rule out more while the conflict continues.

## Why the Bank did it

The Bank's job is to keep inflation close to 2%. Its main tool is the interest rate. Raising it makes borrowing more expensive, so people and businesses spend a little less, and prices rise more slowly.

But the Bank can't make oil cheaper. So why raise rates against an energy shock?

Its real fear is what economists call **second-round effects**. Fuel gets more expensive, so everything costs more. Workers then ask for higher pay to keep up. Businesses raise prices to cover those higher wages. Then workers ask for more again. Once that loop starts, it's hard to stop. The Bank is raising rates now to stop the loop before it starts.

## Why markets barely moved

Two days before the decision, prediction markets put the chance of a rise at about 98.5%. Everyone knew it was coming.

Think of a surprise party where every guest already knows the date and what the cake looks like. When the guest of honour walks in, nobody gasps, because nothing new has happened.

That's what Thursday looked like:

- **The pound** rose to $1.3501 just after the announcement, then gave it all back by Friday. As one analyst at Commerzbank put it, the pound "only benefited briefly and had to give up its gains as the day progressed."
- **Two-year gilt yields**, the interest rate on two-year UK government debt, rose only about 0.03 percentage points to 4.77%.

The lesson is the same one I took from the [US midterms](/blog/what-the-midterms-did-to-the-dollar.html): **markets move on surprises, not on news.** By the time something is news, it's usually already in the price.

## Where the real news was

If the rise wasn't news, what was? Two details:

1. **The vote.** One member wanted a bigger rise. That's a hint about where the next move could go.
2. **The message.** Bailey didn't promise more rises, but he didn't rule them out either.

Here's where the market actually moved. Before Thursday, traders saw roughly a one-in-four chance of another rise in December. Afterwards, it was closer to 50/50. The headline was already priced in. The details changed expectations about the **next** meeting, and that's where the trade was.

## The oil problem

There's a catch in the Bank's forecasts. They assume Brent oil at about $108 a barrel. But on Wednesday, the day before the decision, Brent fell almost 9% to $94.62, as traders bet on an end to the Iran war.

So the Bank raised rates to fight an energy shock that may already be fading. That doesn't make it wrong. The Bank sets policy for the next two years, not the next two days, and oil can rise again as quickly as it fell. But it's why the next two inflation figures matter so much.

## What it means for property

My long-term plan includes investing in property, so I looked at what this rise means there.

Most people on fixed-rate mortgages won't notice anything yet, because their rate is locked in. But anyone on a tracker mortgage, which follows the Bank's rate, pays more straight away: about £37 a month extra on a £250,000 loan, according to Moneyfacts.

For an investor, higher rates do two things. Borrowing costs more, so the rent from a property has to cover a bigger mortgage bill. And fewer people can afford to buy, which can slow house prices. That's a risk for someone who already owns property, and a possible opportunity for someone who's patient.

## Compared with Nigeria

A 4% rate sounds high until you compare it with Nigeria, where the central bank's rate is 23%.

What matters isn't the rate itself, but the rate minus inflation, which economists call the **real rate**. In the UK, that's 4% minus 3.5%, so about 0.5%: savers barely beat rising prices. In Nigeria, it's 23% minus about 15%, so about 8%.

That gap is why foreign investors pour money into the naira to earn that interest, and why that money can leave just as quickly.

## My call

> **The Bank raises rates again, to 4.25%, by its February meeting.**

*Why:* even if oil keeps falling, the damage is already in the pipeline. Inflation is heading towards 5%, and the Bank's biggest fear is that higher prices feed into higher wages. The Bank would rather move early than chase inflation later.

*I'm wrong if:* inflation falls in the next two monthly figures, starting on 18 November, or a lasting ceasefire sends Brent below $90 and keeps it there.

To be honest, this isn't a bold call. After Thursday, markets already expect a rise by February. So the point of writing it down isn't to beat the market. It's to check whether my reasons were the right ones. The next decisions are on 17 December and 4 February, and I'll come back to this either way.

## What I'm taking from it

I expected a big event to cause a big move. But a market doesn't react to how big an event is. It reacts to how different the event is from what it already expected.

> The rise was the headline. The surprise was in the details.
