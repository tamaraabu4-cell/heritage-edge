On Tuesday, the US held its midterm elections. By Wednesday morning, US news organisations projected that Democrats had won control of both the House and the Senate, a bigger swing than markets seemed to expect.

I had a view before the numbers came in: the dollar would fall. It did. But when I checked the evidence, my reason was wrong. This post is about the difference.

## The one limit that matters for markets

The President still has his veto, and overriding it needs two-thirds of both chambers. So most of what the new Congress wants to do, starting with voting to end the tariffs, still needs the President's signature. That shapes almost every move below.

## What moved

Currencies and gold are from Wednesday morning; stocks are from the New York close; oil is from Wednesday afternoon.

- **The dollar index** fell about 0.6% to 101.3, its biggest drop in two months.
- **The euro and the pound** both rose about 0.5% against the dollar.
- **The Mexican peso** gained nearly 1%, one of the biggest winners.
- **The Canadian dollar** rose to a seven-week high.
- **The Swiss franc** gained 0.6%, the best of the major currencies, and the yen rose a little.
- **Gold** rose about 3% to around $4,050, its best day since August. Silver rose 4.6%.
- **Oil** fell sharply. Brent was down about $10 a barrel by the afternoon, below $100 for the first time in weeks, as traders bet on an end to the Iran war.
- **US stocks** fell overnight, then rallied. The Dow closed up 3%, the S&P 500 1.6% and the Nasdaq 1.4%.

## My first view, and why it was wrong

My reason for expecting a weaker dollar was uncertainty: a big political swing should make investors nervous about the US.

But when investors are genuinely scared, they usually **buy** dollars. In 2008 and in March 2020, the dollar rose while almost everything else crashed. So if this had been fear, I'd expect three things: stocks falling, the dollar rising, and risky currencies like the peso falling. All three went the other way.

What about the franc, the yen and gold? They're the classic "safe" assets, and they rose. But look at what else rose against the dollar: the euro, the pound, the peso. When everything rises against one currency, the story is about that currency, not about fear.

There's a simple test. Take a pair with no dollar in it. If investors were running for safety, the franc should have jumped against the euro. Using the morning figures, the euro rose about 0.5% against the dollar and the franc about 0.6%. So the franc gained only about 0.1% against the euro: barely a flicker. **This wasn't fear. It was the dollar being sold.**

## So why was the dollar sold?

**1. The market had already decided.** The dollar started falling before the results came in. Markets move on what they expect, so by the time something is "news", much of it is already in the price.

**2. Slightly lower US interest rates.** The 10-year Treasury yield, the interest rate on ten-year US government debt, edged down to about 4.92%. For currencies, though, the more important number is the **2-year** yield, because it tracks what traders expect the Fed to do next. On Wednesday it moved from 4.61% to 4.58%. Lower rates make holding dollars less rewarding, so some money moves elsewhere.

**3. Gridlock, but it cuts both ways.** Markets often expect a divided government to pass fewer big spending plans, which means less borrowing and lower rates. But the new Congress also wants to scrap tariffs, which raise money for the government, and restore health subsidies. Both would mean *more* borrowing, not less. I think that tension is why the move in interest rates was small, and why the bigger story is elsewhere.

## Gold had two reasons to rise

Gold rose with everything else against the dollar, but it got a double boost:

- **It got cheaper for everyone else.** Gold is priced in dollars, so when the dollar weakens, a buyer paying in euros or pounds gets more gold for the same money.
- **Holding it costs less.** Gold pays no interest. When interest rates edge down, you give up less by holding gold instead of bonds.

## The surprise: the Mexican peso

I didn't expect the peso to be one of the biggest winners. Then it made sense.

Think of a lemonade stand whose biggest customer lives next door. About four-fifths of Mexico's exports go to the US. Tariffs are like the neighbour charging a fee on every cup. Sales fall, fewer dollars come in, and the stand looks less valuable. Now there's talk that the fee might be scrapped:

1. Tariffs might end.
2. Mexican factories could sell more to the US.
3. More dollars would flow into Mexico and be swapped into pesos.
4. So traders buy pesos **now**, before that happens.

The best evidence for this is actually the Canadian dollar. Canada is a big oil exporter, so its currency usually falls when oil falls. On Wednesday, oil dropped about $10 a barrel, and the Canadian dollar still rose to a seven-week high. Something was stronger than oil, and the obvious candidate is the chance that tariffs on Canada and Mexico, the two countries most exposed to them, come down.

Two more things helped the peso. Mexico's interest rate is around 6.5%, well above the Fed's, so investors earn more by holding pesos. That bet does best when investors feel confident, and with stocks up 3%, they did. And the peso is one of the most traded emerging-market currencies, so it's the obvious tool for anyone betting on the end of tariffs.

But remember the veto. The market isn't betting that tariffs end. It's betting on a **higher chance** they end. A price move is roughly **chance × size of the prize**. If tariffs ending for good would push the peso up, say, 10%, then a 1% move suggests traders are pricing only a small chance. That's a cautious bet, not a certain one.

## The weakest story: AI

One explanation was that investors feared a Democratic Congress would regulate AI more tightly, so they sold tech stocks. I'm not convinced:

- **The move started before the result.** Chip stocks had already fallen 12% over the three days before the vote.
- **Nothing is written yet.** Nobody knows what any new rules would look like, and any AI law would face the same veto as the tariff bills.
- **Tech didn't sell off.** The Nasdaq, full of tech companies, closed up 1.4%.

## What it means for the naira

The naira doesn't move freely like the euro or the peso. Nigeria's central bank steers it, so a weaker dollar matters much less here than it does for most currencies. What moves the naira is oil and the central bank, not Washington.

I'm still learning this market, so I went back to basics. Three forces matter most:

1. **Oil.** It's Nigeria's main export, and it's sold in dollars. High oil prices mean more dollars coming in, so the central bank's reserves grow and it can support the naira. Reserves reached about $55bn in late October, built up mainly on oil earnings.
2. **Foreign investors chasing high interest rates.** Nigeria's central bank rate is 23%, against inflation of about 15%. Investors swap dollars into naira, earn that interest, then swap back. It brings in dollars, but it's money that can leave quickly.
3. **The central bank itself.** Its reforms and its selling of dollars to steady the market helped the naira reach a two-year high of about ₦1,332 per dollar in October.

The midterms pull on two of these in opposite directions. Slightly lower US interest rates make Nigeria's 23% look even better, which helps. But one of the Democrats' stated aims is ending the Iran war. A ceasefire would be good news for the world, and it would also take away the "war premium" in oil prices that has been boosting Nigeria's dollar income. On Wednesday, Brent fell about $10 a barrel as traders bet on peace. For the naira, that matters more than any vote in Congress.

## My calls for the end of the year

All three come from one idea. The election moved expectations once, but the Fed sets interest rates every six weeks, and markets were pricing an 81% chance that it **raises** them on 9 December. Higher US rates make the dollar more attractive again, and gold less so. The peso is the exception, because its story is tariffs, not interest rates.

In my [December trade memo](/projects/sofr-fomc-trade-memo.html), I argued the market is too sure about that rise, and put about a 30% chance on a pause. That still leaves a rise as the most likely outcome, so it's my starting point here. But it means the two can clash: if the Fed holds, my trade memo wins and my dollar call probably loses. I'd rather say so than pretend otherwise.

**1. The dollar recovers.** The dollar index ends the year above 102. *Why:* a December rate rise would matter more than a vote. *I'm wrong if:* it falls below 100.5, or the Fed holds on 9 December.

**2. The peso holds its gains.** The peso ends the year no weaker than Wednesday's close, and no more than 2% stronger. *Why:* even talk of ending tariffs helps Mexico, but the veto limits how far that hope can go. *I'm wrong if:* it ends outside that range in either direction.

**3. Gold stays stuck.** Gold has been bouncing around $4,050 for the past year. It ends the year below $4,050 and above $3,900, close to where it was before the vote. *Why:* if the Fed raises rates, the force that lifted gold on Wednesday reverses. *I'm wrong if:* it ends outside that range. The biggest risk is the Iran war escalating, which tends to send investors back into gold.

I'll come back to all three on 31 December and say honestly how they went.

## What I'm taking from it

I got the direction right and the reason wrong. If I'd traded on "uncertainty", I'd have made money on Wednesday and learned nothing, and the next time the dollar rose in a real scare, I'd have lost.

> Being right about the price isn't the same as being right about the reason. Only one of them repeats.
