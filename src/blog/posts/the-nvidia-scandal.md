I don't usually follow individual shares. My focus is currencies, gold and bonds. But for the past week, the Nvidia story has been everywhere, and it's a rare chance to watch one company's share price react, day by day, to a single story. So this is me trying to read it the way I'd read a currency.

This post is about the markets, not anyone's private life. I'll stick to what has been reported and confirmed, and I'll say clearly where something is only alleged. Prices are as of Tuesday's close, plus early trading on Wednesday after the results.

## Why Nvidia matters

Nvidia makes the chips that most of the world's AI runs on. Every company building AI models or data centres buys them. That made it one of the most valuable companies in the world, worth over $5 trillion at its peak in October. It's so big that if you have a pension, you almost certainly own a little of it.

It was also, unusually, still run by the person who started it. Jensen Huang co-founded Nvidia in 1993 and ran it for 33 years. For a lot of investors, he *was* the company.

## What happened

- **29 October:** the New York Times reports that US intelligence officials concluded an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore data centre company and Nvidia customer, was passing information to Beijing, and that she had a "close relationship" with Huang. Nvidia says his meetings with her were "not unusual or unexpected". China calls the reports baseless.
- **5 November:** separately, AI and chip stocks have a bad day, with many falling 9% or more.
- **Sunday 8 November:** Nvidia's board opens an independent investigation into whether Huang was the target of a foreign intelligence effort. In a message to staff, Huang admits he was unfaithful to his wife and says he will cooperate fully. The board says it has "no reason to believe" national security or company secrets were compromised.
- **The same week:** the chairs of three House committees ask Huang to brief them behind closed doors on 18 November.
- **Tuesday 10 November:** a report in The Information claims the relationship helped at least $7.5 billion of Nvidia chips reach Chinese companies since 2023. Reuters couldn't verify it, and it doesn't show that Huang knew about the shipments.
- **Thursday 12 November:** Huang resigns as CEO and leaves the board. The company doesn't give a reason. Colette Kress, the finance chief, becomes interim CEO, and Nvidia gets a board chair for the first time in its history. Megaspeed says it is cooperating with US investigators.
- **The days after:** the New York Times reports that a Commerce Department investigation into Megaspeed was handed to the Justice Department after Nvidia's lawyer contacted investigators. A source tells CNBC the board isn't considering bringing Huang back. Several banks cut their price targets; HSBC's went from $365 to $170.
- **Monday 16 November:** the Commerce Department brings in a new rule requiring a licence to send advanced chips to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil. Its purpose is to close a loophole that let Chinese companies rent Nvidia computing power from data centres in other countries.

Neither the board's investigation nor any government inquiry has reported findings, and nobody has been charged with anything.

## What the share price did

- **Friday 6 November:** $186.64, before the board's investigation.
- **Monday 9 November:** $178.67, down 4.3%.
- **Tuesday 10 November:** $168.89, down 5.5%.
- **Wednesday 11 November:** $173.73, up 2.9%.
- **Thursday 12 November:** $166.19, down 4.3%. In the morning, it had been down 13%.
- **Friday 13 November:** $162.85, down 2%. Its worst week in 19 months.
- **Monday 16 November:** $156.14, down 4.1%.
- **Tuesday 17 November:** $155.46, down 0.4%, before the results that evening.

That's a 17% fall in seven trading days. From its record high on 6 October, the share price is down about 35%, roughly $2 trillion of value.

## Three things the price told me

**1. The market split the company from its industry.**

The most interesting day was Monday. The whole chip sector *rose* 6.9%, bouncing back from the previous week's sell-off. AMD, Nvidia's biggest rival, jumped 13%. Nvidia fell 4.3%.

That tells you investors weren't scared of AI or of chips. They were scared of something specific to Nvidia. Traders split risk into two kinds: risk that hits a whole market (like a recession) and risk that hits one company (like losing its boss). Monday was almost a perfect experiment: same industry, same day, opposite moves. And on resignation day it happened again: Nvidia fell 4.3%, while AMD rose 5.9%. Money didn't leave AI. It moved to Nvidia's competitors.

Then, this Monday, the split disappeared. When the new export rule came out, the whole sector fell together: Nvidia 4.1%, AMD 5.6%, Intel 7%, Micron 7.6%, and the Nasdaq 2.5%. A scandal about one company hits one company. A change in the rules hits everyone.

It also reminded me of my [midterms post](/blog/what-the-midterms-did-to-the-dollar.html), where I argued that the AI sell-off wasn't really about the election. Chip stocks were already falling beforehand. One story rarely explains a whole market. It usually explains one stock.

**2. There are two scandals here, and the market cared more about the second.**

The first is personal: a founder's private life, and a company losing its leader. Traders call that **key person risk**: what happens when one person matters more than the system around them.

The second is about national security: whether chips that the US restricts from China were reaching Chinese buyers, and whether Washington will now tighten the rules. That's a risk to Nvidia's *sales*, not just its leadership. The biggest fall of the week came on Tuesday, the day the export claim came out (hotter-than-expected US inflation didn't help). And it's why a congressional briefing matters as much as a new CEO.

This is a pattern I keep running into. In [Reminiscences of a Stock Operator](/blog/reminiscences-of-a-stock-operator.html), Livermore loses a fortune because the government changes the rules mid-trade. In my [Dangote post](/blog/why-i-did-not-invest-in-dangotes-ipo.html), one of my reasons for not buying was that the company was too tangled up in politics. Nvidia is the same lesson at a much bigger scale: when a company sits at the centre of a fight between two countries, political risk is part of the share price.

**3. By resignation day, a lot was already priced in.**

On Thursday morning, Nvidia was down 13%. By the close, it was down 4.3%. The worst-case news, the founder leaving, arrived, and the share price ended the day only a little below where it closed on Tuesday.

Part of the reason is that it wasn't a full surprise. Reports on Wednesday evening said the board was preparing to remove him. Markets move on surprises, not on news, and by the time the announcement came, much of it was already in the price. The rest of the recovery came from investors deciding that one person leaving doesn't change what Nvidia sells next quarter. The chips are already designed, and customers have already ordered them.

But the calm didn't last. The shares fell on Friday and again on Monday, when the export rule came out. My read: the leadership news was priced in by Thursday. The China news wasn't, and it still isn't.

## Is a company more than its founder?

This is the question the market is really trying to answer.

In my [Arsenal and XTX post](/blog/built-on-defence-arsenal-and-xtx.html), I wrote about two leaders who built something by sticking with their method through the bad years. The difference between a great leader and a great organisation is whether the method survives when the leader goes. Nvidia ran for 33 years without a board chair, with one man as founder, CEO and public face. That makes this a real test.

The case for the company is strong: it still makes the chips everyone needs, and it has years of orders and products in the pipeline. The case against is about the long term: the next big bet, the relationships with customers and governments, and how Washington treats Nvidia now. None of that will show up in one quarter's results.

## What the results showed

On Tuesday evening, Nvidia reported its first results without Huang. The business didn't miss a beat.

- **Revenue:** $115.8 billion for the quarter, more than double a year ago, and about $6 billion more than analysts expected.
- **Data centres**, the AI chips: $107.6 billion, up 110%. Its newest chip system, Vera Rubin, has started shipping.
- **Profit margin:** about 75 cents of every dollar of sales left after the cost of making the chips, slightly better than Nvidia had forecast.
- **Next quarter:** a forecast of about $130 billion, against the $125 billion analysts expected. And that forecast assumes **no data centre sales to China at all**.
- **Next year:** Nvidia raised its growth forecast from about 70% to about 75%.

Kress's message was that it's "business as usual", and that customers are staying. The board has also ruled out bringing Huang back.

The share price reaction was the interesting part. Nvidia closed Tuesday at $155.46, before the results. On Wednesday morning, before the US market opened, it was up about 3.5%, at around $161. AI cloud companies that rent out Nvidia chips rose more, between 6% and 8%.

My take: a $6 billion beat and a bigger forecast moved the shares by about 3.5%, after a fall of 35%. If the market were mainly worried about Nvidia's sales, a result like that should have done much more. So I don't think it's worried about the next quarter. It's worried about Washington, and about who leads the company in five years. Neither was answered on Tuesday night.

The forecast tells me something too. By assuming zero sales to China, Nvidia has already taken the most obvious China risk out of its numbers. What's left is harder to put a number on: the investigations, new rules like Monday's, and whether customers in places like Singapore and Malaysia can keep buying freely.

## Would I buy it?

I don't own Nvidia, and this isn't advice. But I can use the same test I used for Dangote.

At around $160, the shares trade at roughly 20 times the profits analysts expect next year, and that was before Tuesday's beat pushed those expectations up. That's cheaper than they've been for a while, and cheap for a company growing this fast. Greenblatt would say a bad story can make a good business cheap, and that's exactly when to look closely.

But cheap isn't the same as safe. The results answered the question about the business. They didn't answer whether the export claims are true, what Washington will do, or who will run the company. In my Dangote post, I said that supporting a company and owning its shares are two different decisions. Here the gap is different: it's between a great product and a lot of unknowns. For me, the unknowns win for now. I'd rather wait for answers than guess.

## What I'm watching

- **Washington.** The Justice Department's inquiries, any further export rules, and Congress: three House committee chairs have asked Huang for a closed-door interview on 1 December. These matter more for the long-term share price than the leadership story.
- **Profit margins.** Nvidia expects them to fall to 71–72% next quarter, partly because memory chips are getting more expensive. That's the first sign of pressure in the numbers themselves, rather than in the headlines.
- **The investigation's findings, and who becomes permanent CEO.** An internal pick would suggest continuity. An outsider would suggest a reset.

## What I'm taking from it

I expected a story about one person. The share price showed me it was really two stories: a leader leaving, which the market priced quickly, and a political fight over China, which it's still trying to price. The results proved the business works without its founder, for now. They couldn't prove anything about Washington.

> Losing a founder is a shock. Getting caught between two governments is a risk that doesn't go away when the headlines stop.
